I ride a touring bike. I want a race bike for long rides and gran fondos, and I've set myself
a budget of €3,000 to €5,000 for the bike alone.

This post is how I'm deciding. When I've bought one, I'll add what I picked
and why.

## What I want

- **Comfort over long hours** matters most.
- **Electronic shifting**, if the budget allows.
- **Best value.** I don't mind whether it comes from a shop or straight from
  the brand online.

I haven't had a bike fit, so getting the size right matters (more on that below).

## What actually matters

### 1. Fit, before anything else

Frame sizes like "54" or "M" mean different things at different brands. What
matters are two numbers: **stack** (how high the handlebars are) and
**reach** (how far forward). An endurance bike has more stack and less reach
than a pure race bike, so you sit more upright. Coming from a touring bike,
that's what I want.

My plan: use Canyon's size advice for my height and inseam, and lean on its
30-day return if the size turns out wrong.

<Expand kind="deep" title="Stack and reach, explained" teaser="Why '54' on one bike isn't '54' on another" time="1 min">

Stack is the vertical distance from the middle of the cranks to the top of
the head tube. Reach is the horizontal distance between the same two points.
Two bikes with the same stack and reach will put you in roughly the same
position, whatever their size labels say.

If you're between two sizes on an endurance bike, the larger one is usually
the safer choice. Don't count on a long stem or a stack of spacers to fix a
frame that's the wrong size.

Source: [BikeTips on stack and reach](https://biketips.com/bike-stack-and-reach-explained/).

</Expand>

### 2. Endurance geometry and comfort tech

Every bike on my list is an "endurance" bike: a race bike with a slightly
taller, shorter front, longer wheelbase and room for wide tyres. Some add
comfort tech on top:

- **Specialized Roubaix:** a small suspension unit under the handlebars
  (about 20 mm of travel).
- **Trek Domane:** IsoSpeed, which lets the seat tube flex.
- **Canyon Endurace:** a seatpost designed to flex, and a cockpit you can
  adjust in width and height.
- **Giant Defy:** a flexy seatpost and handlebar.

### 3. Wide tyres (and room for mudguards)

Wider tyres at lower pressure are more comfortable, and not slower: in one
lab test, 32 mm tyres rolled at least 5% more efficiently than otherwise
identical 30 mm ones. (The test was paid for by the tyre maker, so take the
exact number lightly.) Every bike here comes with 32 mm tyres.

The catch for Dutch winters: mudguards take up space. Check the clearance
*with* mudguards fitted, not the headline number.

### 4. Electronic shifting is the default now

At this price, every bike on my list has electronic shifting: Shimano 105
Di2 or SRAM Rival AXS, or better. Mechanical versions start around €3,000.
So the real trade-off isn't shifting, it's **wheels**: some bikes come with
carbon wheels, others with aluminium ones and the money spent on comfort tech
instead.

### 5. Online or a shop?

Brands that sell direct (Canyon, Decathlon's Van Rysel) give you more bike
per euro, and Canyon has a 30-day return. But you build it yourself, and
some parts are proprietary. A shop gives you a test ride, a fitting and
someone to fix it. Stock in the Netherlands is patchy right now, so it's worth
checking with a shop before relying on a web price.

## The one I lean towards

The **Canyon Endurace CF SLX 8 Di2**, because of what you get for the money.
At €4,499 it costs the same as the Specialized Roubaix SL8 Comp, but comes
with:

- **Shimano Ultegra Di2** instead of SRAM Rival AXS, one level up;
- **carbon wheels** (DT Swiss, 35 mm deep) instead of aluminium ones;
- a **power meter** (4iiii) on the cranks, according to Canyon's page;
- a claimed **8.1 kg** in size M.

Compared with the SLX 7 Di2 from the same frame family, the extra €500 buys
the Ultegra groupset, the power meter and about 340 g less weight. Same
frame, wheels and tyre clearance.

Two catches:

- It's **"coming soon"** on Canyon's site: no size has a delivery date yet.
- Its cassette is **11-34**, against 11-36 on the SLX 7, so the easiest gear
  is a little harder. Fine for the Netherlands; worth knowing for mountains.

## What's next

1. Wait for the SLX 8 Di2 to be in stock.
2. Order it online in the size Canyon recommends.
3. Ride it, and update this post.
